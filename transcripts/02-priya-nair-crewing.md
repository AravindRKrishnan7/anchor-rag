title: Building and keeping good crews
date: 2026-09-22

Interviewer: What is the hardest part of crewing a fleet today?
Priya Nair: Keeping the good people. Finding officers is possible if you pay enough, but getting them to come back for a second and third contract is the real work. Our turnover used to be close to thirty percent a year, and every replacement costs us in training and in mistakes.

Interviewer: Why do officers not come back?
Priya Nair: Mostly it is how they were treated on board and how long they were kept there. If a contract stretches from six months to nine because we could not arrange a reliever, that officer remembers it. Late wages and a bad captain do the same damage.

Interviewer: How serious is fatigue on your ships?
Priya Nair: More serious than the paperwork shows. On short coastal runs the officers can have three port calls in two days, with cargo watches in between. The rest-hour records often look perfect because people adjust them to avoid trouble. We started comparing them with the port call times, and the gaps were obvious.

Interviewer: What did you change after that?
Priya Nair: We added a third officer on the busiest coastal vessels so watches could be split properly, and we told masters that an honest record showing a breach will not be punished. A falsified one will. It took a year for people to believe us.

Interviewer: What about certificates and training?
Priya Nair: STCW compliance is the floor, not the goal. I track every certificate expiry in the fleet and book refresher courses three months ahead, because the training institutes in Kochi fill up quickly before the monsoon.
