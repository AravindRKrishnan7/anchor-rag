Interviewer: What does a marine pilot actually do?
Asha Rao: I board ships outside the harbour and take them in or out of port. The captain stays in command, but I bring the local knowledge: the channel, the currents, the depth at each state of tide and how the tugs here work. A master who has never called at Kochi relies on that for the last few miles.

Interviewer: How do you get on board?
Asha Rao: By pilot ladder, usually from a launch in open water. In the monsoon that is the most dangerous part of the job. The launch is rising and falling two metres and you step across at the top of the swell. A badly rigged ladder is my biggest worry, so I check the ropes and the spreaders before I touch it, and I refuse a ladder that is not safe.

Interviewer: What makes Kochi difficult for big ships?
Asha Rao: The channel is narrow and the cross-current at the entrance can set a ship sideways by several metres a minute. With a large container ship we plan the turn before she even reaches the breakwater, and we keep the speed up so the rudder still bites. Slowing down too early is the classic mistake.

Interviewer: How do you work with the ship's crew?
Asha Rao: The first five minutes on the bridge matter most. I give the master my plan, ask about the engine and any defects, and agree how we will talk to each other. If the officer of the watch is quiet I ask him to repeat my orders back. Most near misses I have seen began with a misunderstood order, not with bad seamanship.

Interviewer: What advice would you give someone who wants to become a pilot?
Asha Rao: Get your sea time as a deck officer first and do it on different kinds of ships. Pilots are chosen for judgement, and judgement comes from having seen a lot of ships behave badly. Then learn one port deeply. I still find something new in this harbour every season.
